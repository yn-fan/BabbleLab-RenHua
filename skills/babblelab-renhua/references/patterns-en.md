# English editing patterns

Use this reference for English prose and English passages within mixed-language text. These are editing cues, not evidence of who wrote a passage. Apply a change when it improves meaning or readability in context; quoted wording, deliberate voice, and a required format can justify keeping a pattern.

The source numbers below refer to Humanizer 3.1.0. Groups consolidate its 26 patterns without making words or punctuation a blacklist. Preserve the source's claims, qualifications, quantities, attribution, and relationships through every edit.

## 1. Staged openings and artificial contrasts

Source: §1, §3, §4, §5.

Lead with the claim when a theatrical opening, vague aphorism, or imagined objection merely delays it. A contrast is useful when it corrects a real misconception or both sides supply information. Preserve an objection that the author actually answers, including its attribution; keep a metaphor that conveys a specific meaning.

Before: “Here's the thing. The report isn't just available online; it is available online and in print.”

After: “The report is available online and in print.”

## 2. Repeated conclusions and dramatic fragments

Source: §2.

Remove a closer that only repeats the preceding point or tells the reader how impressed to be. Keep a short sentence when it adds a consequence, a decision, or intentional emphasis. An ending need not become a lesson, slogan, or invitation.

Before: “The room has 12 desks. Twelve desks. Let that sink in.”

After: “The room has 12 desks.”

## 3. Mechanical rhythm and lists

Source: §6, §7.

Read adjacent sentences together. Merge redundant items and vary structure when repetition obscures the point. Keep three items when there are three distinct ideas; preserve sequence, separate actors, and simultaneity where they matter. Deliberate repetition in a speech or personal passage can remain.

Before: “The review was careful, meticulous, and thorough. It found two missing dates.”

After: “The thorough review found two missing dates.”

## 4. Punctuation and English typography

Source: §8, §10, §21.

Choose punctuation to express the relationship between clauses. Repeated dashes may need a paragraph rewrite, but an isolated dash is not a defect. Use the author's or publisher's convention for quotation marks and compound modifiers; consult spelling or style guidance when needed. Preserve ranges, quotations, identifiers, and technical syntax. These English conventions do not determine Chinese punctuation.

Before: “The update — released Tuesday — changes two labels.”

After: “Released Tuesday, the update changes two labels.”

Both versions are valid. Prefer the second only if it fits the surrounding rhythm.

## 5. Qualifications, subjects, and passive voice

Source: §9, §11.

Remove redundant uncertainty without strengthening the claim. Keep limits such as “may,” “up to,” “in this sample,” and “according to” when they carry meaning. Name an actor when the source supplies one and doing so clarifies the action. Passive voice remains useful when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or intentionally backgrounded; do not invent one.

Before: “The service could potentially be unavailable for up to five minutes.”

After: “The service could be unavailable for up to five minutes.”

## 6. Inflated vocabulary and indirect verbs

Source: §12, §18.

Prefer a concrete verb or familiar word when it says the same thing. “Is” and “has” often work, but precise technical terms and the author's meaningful word choices take priority. Inspect clusters of vague vocabulary rather than substituting every word on a list.

Before: “This folder serves as the location for three sample files.”

After: “This folder contains three sample files.”

## 7. Promotional claims and attached significance

Source: §13, §15, §16.

Separate the actual statement from praise or an attached phrase that merely restates it. Retain an author's stated evaluation when it is part of their position. Preserve supported consequences and future plans. If a substantial claim lacks enough context to rewrite safely, retain its qualification or flag the gap instead of inventing supporting detail.

Before: “The update adds offline search, showcasing the addition of offline search.”

After: “The update adds offline search.”

## 8. Attribution and vague relationships

Source: §14, §17.

Use the relationship and source actually given. Do not turn “associated with” into “founded,” an unnamed view into a research finding, or a media mention into an endorsement. Lack of a citation does not by itself authorize deleting a claim. Retain names, counts, and attributed opinions unless the user requested a summary that permits omission; flag unresolved sourcing separately when relevant.

Before: “Mira is associated with the committee as its secretary. According to the committee's report, attendance rose from 40 to 52.”

After: “Mira is the committee's secretary. The committee's report says attendance rose from 40 to 52.”

## 9. Decorative structure and repeated labels

Source: §19, §20, §24.

Use headings, emphasis, and lists to support scanning or comparison. Remove a label or opening sentence when it repeats the same information. Retain meaningful labels, real distinctions, accessibility needs, and a requested template. Do not force a useful table or checklist into paragraphs.

Before: “**Deadline:** The deadline is Friday. **Cost:** The cost is $18.”

After: “The deadline is Friday. The cost is $18.”

## 10. Chat residue, document narration, and the reader's context

Source: §22, §23, §25, §26.

For a standalone artifact, remove an assistant's greeting, offer to continue, or account of how it drafted the text. Keep genuine correspondence greetings and sign-offs. Preserve source limitations that affect the reader's interpretation. If the source contains speculation, preserve its qualification and flag any sourcing gap; remove speculation introduced by the editor. When the surrounding conversation is available, lead a reply with the decision and avoid repeating context the recipient already supplied. Keep evidence, provenance, migration history, and instructions when the document needs them.

Before: “Great question! Here is the schedule: registration opens on 4 May. I hope this helps!”

After: “Registration opens on 4 May.”

For factual gaps, “The supplied report does not give a launch date” should remain equally limited. Do not turn it into “The launch date is unknown.” For personal writing, retain the author's stated experiences and reactions; do not manufacture a first-person voice.
